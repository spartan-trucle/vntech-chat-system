Requirements, and why each one is there

A requirement without a reason is a requirement you can't push back on. The "why" column is what the requirement is protecting; the "challenge" column is the question that would remove it or change its shape. If a challenge wins, the spec changes.

ID
Requirement
Why it's here
Challenge this
F1

One-to-one chat

Two users exchange text in real time.

Why

The base case every other flow degrades to. If this isn't fast and durable, nothing else matters.

Challenge

Is 1:1 just a group of two? If yes, do we design one path or two?

F2

Group chat, up to 100 members

One message, many recipients, each with several devices.

Why

Forces the fan-out decision. At 100 members × 1.5 devices one send becomes 150 deliveries.

Challenge

Why 100 and not 500 or 100 000? The ceiling decides whether fan-out-on-write survives.

F3

Real-time delivery

p50 under 100 ms, p99 under 500 ms, both parties online.

Why

Sets the protocol: anything with request overhead per message fails the p50.

Challenge

Measured from where to where? Device-to-device includes the mobile radio; we can't control that.

F4

Offline delivery, notify, mark unread

Messages to an offline user wait, then arrive on reconnect. Every new message pushes a notification and marks the conversation unread until opened.

Why

Separates "delivered to a server" from "delivered to a person". Introduces the per-user inbox and the per-conversation unread state.

Challenge

Five devices online: who gets the push, and does opening on one clear the badge on the others?

F5

Persistent history

Any device can page back through the conversation.

Why

The write-heavy, read-recent access pattern is what picks the database.

Challenge

Does history belong on the server at all? WhatsApp historically kept it on the device.

F6

Multi-device sync

Up to 5 devices per user share one conversation state.

Why

Turns "deliver to user" into "deliver to each device cursor". This is the subtle one.

Challenge

Do read receipts sync across the reader's own devices, or only to the sender?

F7

Online indicator, must have

Online, offline, or last seen, for the people on screen. Detected within 10 s, visible to watchers within 15 s, 5 s debounce.

Why

Presence is the most expensive cheap-looking feature, but the cost is in propagation, not detection.

Challenge

Answered: delivered by subscription only (argument 10). Still worth asking: does any screen need presence for people not on it?

F8

Message status, per member

Sent, delivered, read. In groups the sender sees who read, not only how many.

Why

Each transition is an event flowing backwards. Per member in a 100-group is 100 read events per message unless they are coalesced.

Challenge

Store a receipt per message per member, or one read cursor per member per conversation? Decided: cursor. See design.md §3.

F9

Media up to 10 MB, server never processes it

Images, video, files. No server-side pipeline; the client compresses images by default and may send originals. One per message.

Why

Blob bytes dwarf text bytes by orders of magnitude and must never ride the real-time path. "No pipeline" keeps a second system out of this design.

Challenge

Answered: "full size" meant simplicity, not fidelity, so client compression is in (argument 11). Still open: should the 10 % media rate be challenged too?

F11

Auto-reconnect

A client reconnects by itself when the network returns and resumes with no gap and no duplicates.

Why

Mobile radios drop constantly. Without this, "real-time" is only true on Wi-Fi.

Challenge

Already designed in design.md §1.4: jittered backoff, device cursor, client message ID for dedup. Is there a case it misses?

F10

Quoted reply

A message references one earlier message in the same conversation. Threads stay out.

Why

Cheap to add, but it is the first feature where a message depends on another message.

Challenge

Copy the quoted text into the reply, or fetch by ID? Deleted parents decide this.

Choosing the stack, one layer at a time

Each layer gets the question it has to answer, the candidates, the criteria that actually discriminate, a lean, and the best argument against that lean. "Open" means we have not argued it yet. A layer only turns "decided" after the counterpoint has been answered in writing.

The high-level picture these layers add up to is in the architecture diagram (interactive; use the three guided views for the send path, the offline path, and history and media).

Real-time transport

decided

How do 10 M clients hold a connection and receive pushes without polling?

Criteria: bidirectional, works through corporate proxies and mobile carriers, reconnect cost, library maturity on iOS, Android and web.

WebSocketBidirectional, universal client support, one TCP connection per device. Needs explicit heartbeat and reconnect logic.
Long pollingWorks everywhere, but one HTTP request per message and per timeout; p50 target is hard to hit.
Server-sent events + HTTPPush only; sends go over separate requests. Simpler, but two paths to reason about.
MQTT / XMPPPurpose-built messaging protocols. Great fit, smaller talent pool, heavier client story on web.
Decision

WebSocket for the real-time path; plain HTTPS for login, history paging and media. Server-initiated ping every 5 s, two misses = dead (10 s, matching F7); client reconnects with jittered backoff and resumes from its device cursor. Heartbeats never touch Redis. Full write-up: design.md §1.

Counterpoint, answered

"You end up implementing half of MQTT yourself." True, and §1 is that half: ping/pong, resume cursor, client-side dedup key. It is about 200 lines per platform, and we keep the ubiquity of WebSocket on web.

Chat server runtime

decided for the prototype

What holds millions of idle connections cheaply and routes messages between them?

Criteria: memory per idle connection, scheduler behaviour under 100 k connections per node, what the team can operate at 3 a.m.

Kotlin on Netty (Ktor / Micronaut)The team's stack. Netty handles the connection count; the JVM wants tuning for memory per socket.
Elixir / Erlang (BEAM)The textbook answer. WhatsApp and Discord's gateway. Per-connection processes, built-in supervision. New language for the team.
GoGoroutine per connection, small memory footprint, simple deploys. No actor model; you build supervision yourself.
Node.js + TypeScriptFastest to prototype; ws exposes ping/pong directly. Single-threaded, so it proves the protocol, not the connection count.
Decision

Node.js with TypeScript for the prototype, because the prototype proves protocol correctness and that is the fastest path. The production runtime is deliberately not decided; Kotlin on Netty remains the default for real work. Argument 7 below.

Counterpoint, parked

BEAM is the language this problem was shaped by, and a Node prototype teaches nothing about connections per node. Both true, both outside the prototype's stated goal.

Message store

decided

What absorbs 115 k writes per second and serves "last 50 messages in this conversation" and "everything since cursor X"?

Criteria: write throughput without a single primary, partition by conversation, range scan by message ID, 730 TB of growth, operational burden.

Cassandra / ScyllaDBPartition on conversation, cluster on message ID. Discord's path. Wide partitions and tombstones are the known traps.
PostgreSQL, partitioned + shardedFamiliar, transactional, excellent tooling. Sharding by conversation is manual work and the ceiling arrives at the primary.
DynamoDBSame data model as Cassandra, zero operations, pay per request. Cost at 2 B writes/day is a real line item.
HBaseFacebook Messenger's original choice. Strong ordering per row, heavier to run than the rest.
Decision

Wide-column, partition key (conversation, month bucket), clustering by message ID descending. All history kept for 5 years; open loads the newest 50, scroll-up pages by cursor over HTTPS, never over the socket. Two cursors per device: oldest-loaded (back) and last-synced (forward). Full write-up: design.md §2.

Counterpoint, parked

"Nobody has run Cassandra in anger" is a real risk, but the frame chosen is the canonical design, so the practice is to learn it. Revisit if the frame changes.

Message ID

decided

How does every message get an ID that is unique, sortable within a conversation, and mintable at 115 k/s with no coordinator?

Snowflake-style (time + node + sequence)64-bit, roughly time-ordered, generated locally. Clock skew between nodes is the risk.
Per-conversation sequencePerfect ordering within a chat. Needs a counter per conversation, which is a hot key for busy groups.
Database-assignedSimple, but ties ID minting to the store's write path and its availability.
Decision

Snowflake minted in the chat server; the timestamp inside the ID also selects the month bucket for pagination, so a scroll-up cursor needs no lookup.

Counterpoint, accepted

Two messages in the same millisecond from different servers may swap. A quoted reply carries its parent ID explicitly, so the chain is still correct even if display order flips.

Routing, presence and session state

decided

When a message arrives for Khanh, which server holds Khanh's three connections, and is Khanh online right now?

Criteria: sub-millisecond lookups, TTL-based expiry for heartbeats, pub/sub for presence fan-out, survives a node loss without a thundering reconnect.

Redis (cluster)Session map with TTL, presence with TTL, pub/sub for fan-out. One system, well understood. Not a durable store.
etcd / ZooKeeperRight for server discovery, wrong for 10 M user sessions churning every few seconds.
In-memory on the chat server + gossipNo external dependency, but every server must know every other's users. Does not scale past a few nodes.
Decision

Redis for the routing table and presence state with a slow lease (2 min renew, 5 min TTL); detection is local to the chat server; presence pushed only to subscribed watchers via a pub/sub channel per user. etcd or the orchestrator for chat-server discovery only. Argument 10 below.

Counterpoint, answered

"10 M presence writes per heartbeat interval." Only if the lease is tied to detection; it is not. Heartbeats never touch Redis, and the lease is a safety net behind service discovery, so it renews every 2 minutes.

Fan-out and queueing

decided

Between "stored" and "on the recipient's screen", is there a queue, and who consumes it?

KafkaDurable, replayable, partition per user or per conversation. Adds latency and a large operational surface.
Redis Streams / pub-subChosen as the queue backing with a 24 h window. Losing a shard sends its devices to the slow path, which is rate-limited for that reason.
Direct push + per-device sync queue on Redis StreamsThe textbook path: store, push straight to online recipients' servers, and append to each device's sync queue for cursor catch-up and offline delivery.
NATS JetStreamLighter than Kafka, durable streams, less ecosystem.
Decision

Direct push for online recipients; per-device sync queue on Redis Streams with a 24 h window for catch-up; slow path from the store beyond that. Backpressure: bounded per-destination buffer, drop to the device's catch-up path on timeout. Arguments 6, 8 and 9 below.

Counterpoint, answered

"Redis is not durable." Correct, and the queue is a fast path, not a source of truth: the message store is. A shard loss costs latency for its devices, bounded by rate-limiting the slow path.

Media storage and delivery

decided

Where do 400 TB a day of full-size blobs live, and how do 100 recipients fetch the same 10 MB file?

Object storage + CDN, pre-signed URLsClient uploads straight to the bucket, chat server never touches bytes. Short-lived signed URLs handle authorization.
Upload through the API serversCentral place to enforce the 10 MB limit and scan content. Every byte crosses your fleet twice.
Peer-to-peer / device relayZero server storage; falls apart the moment the sender goes offline.
Decision

Direct-to-bucket upload with a pre-signed URL that carries the size cap; message sent after upload completes. Client compresses images by default. Regional buckets; CDN in front of group media only. Argument 11 below.

Counterpoint, answered

"A CDN is pure cost for 1:1." Agreed, which is why the URL host is chosen per conversation type: bucket for 1:1, CDN for groups.

Push notifications

decided

How does an offline phone learn there is something waiting?

APNs + FCM via a notification serviceThe only way onto the device when the app is closed. Carry a hint, never the message body.
Decision

There is no real alternative; the only questions are what the payload contains and how to avoid notifying five devices for one message.

Still worth arguing

Should the push carry the message text for the lock screen preview, given it leaves our infrastructure?

Arguments and decisions, in the order we had them

This is the record of the debate so far: what was asked, what each side said, and what we settled or left open. Read it top to bottom and you have the reasoning behind every "decided" badge on the board above. Full write-ups with numbers live in design.md.

1. Canonical design, or what we would actually run?decided: canonical

The first question on the page, because it sets the frame for every other one. "Canonical" means the architecture in Alex Xu's System Design Interview chapter 12: stateless API servers, stateful chat servers over WebSocket, a presence service, a wide-column message store, a per-user message sync queue, Snowflake IDs, push notifications.

Why canonicalThis is practice. The point is to learn why chat systems look the way they do, and the textbook is the distilled version of WhatsApp, Messenger and Discord. Picking the familiar stack would be picking the lesson we skip.
Why "what we'd run"A Kotlin and Postgres system we can operate at 3 a.m. beats a Cassandra cluster we misconfigure. Practice that does not transfer to work is entertainment.

Decided: canonical. This closed four layers at once: WebSocket transport, wide-column store, Snowflake IDs, direct push with a sync queue. It did not close the programming language, because the book never names one.

2. Cassandra over Postgres: the five questionsdecided: wide-column

Truc asked five pointed questions about the store. The answers are the real argument behind the decision.

Fast write and read?
The pattern is append-heavy, read-recent, almost never update. Cassandra writes go to a commit log plus an in-memory table: no B-tree to rebalance, no primary to funnel through, so write throughput scales with node count. Postgres reaches 115 k writes/s only by sharding by conversation, and every shard still has one primary as the ceiling. Reads of "newest 50 in this conversation" are fast on both; the write path and the growth to 730 TB are what differ.
Do we need transactions?
A message insert touches one row. The only multi-row operation, "store message, then append to N sync queues", is deliberately not atomic: partial fan-out is retried idempotently because the message ID is already fixed. Group membership changes are rare enough for a lightweight transaction or an outbox. We give up cross-row ACID because the domain does not need it.
Where are we in CAP and ACID?
Cassandra is AP: quorum writes on replication factor 3 survive one node loss and are readable immediately by a quorum reader. We trade linearizability for availability, which is what chat wants: a partition should degrade to "your message is delayed", never "you cannot send". The price is duplicates, which is why every message carries a client-generated ID for dedup. On ACID we keep atomicity and durability per row, and isolation only within a partition. That is exactly the scope we use.
Why sort by Snowflake ID and not created_at?
You can, but you pay twice. Two messages in the same millisecond in one conversation collide unless you add a tiebreaker, so the key becomes (created_at, message_id) anyway. And a timestamp makes an ambiguous pagination cursor: before=21:03:00.417 cannot say which of three messages at that millisecond it means. A Snowflake ID is timestamp plus tiebreaker in one 64-bit integer, so it is both the sort key and the cursor. The cost is clock skew between chat servers, accepted because a quoted reply carries its parent ID explicitly and NTP keeps skew under human perception. created_at stays as a plain column for display and bucket selection.
Multiple chat servers: do we need sticky sessions?
Yes, but not in the load-balancer sense. A WebSocket is one TCP connection, so it is stuck to one server by nature; the balancer only picks a server at connect time. What we need is the inverse lookup: when a message arrives for Khanh, which server holds Khanh's three connections? That is the Redis routing table, route:{user}:{device} → server. Sticky by connection, stateless on the API layer, shared routing map for cross-server delivery. The thing we must not do is pin a user to a server across reconnects, because that turns one node's crash into a permanent outage for its users.

Decided: wide-column store, partition key (conversation, month bucket), clustering by Snowflake ID descending. The "nobody here has run Cassandra in anger" risk is real and parked under the canonical frame.

3. Heartbeat, disconnect detection, auto-reconnectdecided

Three jobs are usually conflated: keep idle sockets alive through NATs and carriers, detect an unclean drop so presence can mark the user offline, and let the client notice a half-open socket. A clean close is detected instantly; the heartbeat exists for the unclean case.

DecisionServer-initiated WebSocket ping frames every 5 s, two misses means dead (about 10 s). Client mirror rule: no ping for 15 s, close and reconnect with jittered backoff from 1 s to a 30 s cap. The hello frame on reconnect carries the device cursor, so the server replays what was missed before live traffic. Outgoing messages are written to a local outbox with a client-generated ID and flushed on reconnect; the server dedups for 24 h.
The cost, namedThe 5 s interval exists only because F7 says presence changes must be visible within 10 s. Relaxing F7 to 30 s makes the ping 15 s and cuts fleet heartbeat traffic three times. This is the cheapest lever on the page and should be re-asked before launch.

The insight that makes the interval affordable: heartbeats never write to Redis. Pongs update a timestamp in the chat server's memory. Presence in Redis is a lease renewed once every 30 s per server in one pipelined batch, so a node with 100 k connections does 3.3 k key writes a second instead of 20 k. A crashed server's leases expire in 60 s; service discovery also watches server liveness and marks that server's users offline faster.

Decided. Why server-initiated protocol pings: browsers cannot send ping frames but answer them for free, pong is handled below application backpressure, and the server owns the clock.

4. History: keep everything, load the newest, page on scrolldecided

All messages are kept server-side, forever. Opening a conversation loads the newest 50; scrolling up pages older ones by cursor over plain HTTPS, never over the WebSocket, because history is a cacheable read path with its own scaling and a deep scroll by one user must not compete with live delivery for everyone on that node.

Partition by (conversation, month)
A 100-member group doing 10 k messages a day is 300 k rows a month, about 60 MB, well under the 100 MB partition guideline. Without the bucket, a five-year-old group is one multi-GB partition, the classic Cassandra failure.
Cursor, not offset
?before={message_id}&limit=50. The Snowflake timestamp selects the month bucket, so a cursor needs no lookup. Offsets are O(n) in a wide-column store and break when messages arrive while the user scrolls.
Two cursors per device per conversation
oldest_loaded moves backwards for scroll-up over HTTPS. last_synced moves forward for new messages over the socket. They never meet.
Jump to a quoted parent outside the window
Fetch by key, then page around it. Not a scroll-up loop.

Decided. Rejected: load full history on open (p50 impossible), offset paging, history over the socket, device-only history (fails new-device and lost-phone cases).

5. Read receipts per member, unread state, notificationsdecided

Truc wanted per-member read tracking in groups, unread marking on every new message, and a push notification. Per member is the expensive choice if done naïvely: 100 read events per message in a full group, each fanned out to 150 devices, is 15 000 receipt frames for one message and 100 receipt rows for every message row.

What makes it affordableReading is monotonic. A member who read message 700 has read everything before it. So the state is not "who read message N" but "how far has each member read": one last_read_id per member per conversation, updated with max(), which is idempotent and order-independent. A message's status is derived by comparing cursors, never stored. Receipts are coalesced to one per conversation per 2 s and pushed only to members with that conversation on screen.
What it costs"Who has read" is a partition read plus a compare, not a stored list, so the UI does one small query when the sender opens the receipt sheet. Acceptable: nobody opens that sheet 100 times a second.

Unread is derived the same way: last_message_id(conversation) > last_read_id(conversation, user). One read cursor per user, not per device, is what makes reading on the phone clear the badge on the laptop. Notifications go only to devices without a live socket, carry a hint and never the body, and go to all offline devices of the user so the read-cursor push clears the rest.

Decided. Rejected: receipt row per message per member, pushing receipts to everyone, per-device read cursors, pushing message bodies, a stored unread counter (hot key, drifts on retry).

6. Fan-out and queueing, explaineddirection decided; backing open

This is the part between "the chat server has stored the message" and "it is on every recipient's screen". Two decisions hide in it.

Who does the delivery work, and when
Fan-out on write: the sender's chat server does all the work at send time, looks up every recipient device, pushes to each one's server. Fan-out on read: write once, recipients pull when they look. For groups of 100, on write: 150 pushes is small and recipients expect messages without asking. The flip point is the large-channel follow-up: at 100 k members one send is 150 k pushes, and channels switch to fan-out on read.
Is there a queue between the store and the screen
If every message passes through a durable queue before reaching an online recipient, you pay the queue's latency on every delivery; a Kafka p99 alone can be most of the 500 ms budget. The textbook splits the cases: online recipient, push directly server to server, no queue in the path; everything else, meaning offline, reconnecting, and "did I miss anything", is served by a per-device sync queue drained from the device cursor. The queue is for catch-up, not the hot path.
What the sync queue actually is
Not a work queue with consumers. An ordered log per device of message IDs not yet acknowledged, read only by that device, trimmed as its cursor advances.
Backpressure, the counterpoint still on the board
Direct push means one slow recipient server in a 100-member group can stall the sender's server. The fix is a bounded per-destination buffer with a drop-to-queue rule: if the recipient server does not take the push within budget, the message stays in that device's sync queue and the device catches up on its own. Backpressure becomes a delay for one recipient, not a stall for the sender.

Decided across arguments 6, 8 and 9: direct push plus a per-device sync queue on Redis Streams with a 24-hour window.

7. Chat-server runtimedecided: Node.js for the prototype

The first unanswered question after the canonical frame. Before comparing the three, the challenge on the table is whether the decision is load-bearing at all.

The challengeEvery decision so far shows up in an artifact: the store shapes the schema, the transport shapes the heartbeat protocol, the ID shapes the cursor. The runtime shapes none of them; all three candidates hold 100 k sockets per node. So: what will be different in this repository depending on the runtime? If the answer is "a line in design.md", the verdict is "revisit when there is code" and the hour goes to presence fan-out instead.
When it would matterIf the exercise ends in a prototype chat server to test the heartbeat design, the runtime matters a lot, and the argument becomes "which runtime teaches the most per week of practice", not "which is best for WhatsApp". Then BEAM is the strongest candidate precisely because it is the one nobody would pick at work.

Truc answered: there will be a prototype, its job is to prove the heartbeat, reconnect and replay protocol, not connection count, and the shortlist is Kotlin or Node.js with "fastest" as the criterion.

Decided: Node.js with TypeScript and ws for the prototype. Protocol-level ping/pong is exposed directly, one file runs in minutes, and the load-balancer repo already uses the same tooling. Kotlin costs half a day of framework setup before the first line of protocol logic. Accepted risks: nothing transfers to the Kotlin stack, and a single-threaded Node process says nothing about connections per node; if that question becomes real, it gets its own prototype. The production runtime is a separate decision. ADR: .memory/decisions/2026-11-01-prototype-runtime-nodejs.md.

8. Does the sync queue need to exist?decided: keep it

Asked as "Cassandra, Redis Streams, or Kafka for the sync queue", answered with a prior question.

The challengeAfter arguments 4 and 5 we already have: the message store ordered by ID per conversation, conv:{id}:last in Redis, and a cursor per device per conversation. "What did this device miss" can be answered by listing the user's conversations, comparing each one's last ID to the device cursor, and paging the store forward for the ones that are behind. The sync queue is a second write of every message times 150 devices in a full group, roughly 300 billion appends a day, into a structure that exists only to be trimmed. What does it give a reconnecting device that "compare cursors, then page the store" does not?
The one defence so farA user in 500 conversations makes the comparison itself expensive on every reconnect, and mobile reconnects are constant. If that is the case being protected, the argument is about the threshold where a queue beats a scan, not about which product backs the queue.

Truc's answer settled it from the product side: on reconnect the chat list must show which conversations have new messages immediately, and open chats must then be complete. Those are two different reads. "Compare cursors, then page the store" is the right answer for the first one and the wrong answer for the second: rebuilding a week of messages across 200 conversations is 200 range scans stitched into one ordered stream on the client, which is exactly what the queue already is.

Decided: keep the per-device sync queue (IDs only, ~50 bytes a row, 30-day TTL, replayed from the device cursor), and add a conversation summary read over HTTPS that renders the chat list with unread dots and previews before a single queued message has replayed. Sequence and bounds in design.md §4. Still open: what backs the queue, once the write volume is estimated.

9. What backs the sync queue, and where do cursors livedecided

Asked as "Cassandra, Redis Streams, or Kafka". The opening challenge was that the 30-day window, a placeholder, was deciding the answer: 54 TB of live data forces Cassandra or Kafka to serve the small fraction of reconnects the slow path already handles correctly.

Truc's answerThe fast path serves 24 hours of offline. Longer than that, the device accepts loading from the persistent store. And the follow-up question that mattered: should cursors always live in Redis or Kafka, and what happens when they are lost?
The constraint that answers itAt a 24 h window the live data is ~1.8 TB, which fits a Redis cluster of about ten nodes. Redis Streams gives exactly the three operations the queue needs: XADD on fan-out, XRANGE from the cursor on replay, XTRIM by time. Neither Cassandra's tombstone care nor Kafka's consumer model is needed for a one-day fast path.
Cursors: owner, durable copy, loss
The device's sync cursor is owned by the device and sent on every connect; the server never stores it, so there is no second copy to disagree with. The user's read cursor is owned by the server because other people read it; it lives in Cassandra with Redis as a cache. Kafka is not a cursor store at all: its offsets name positions in Kafka, not message IDs in our store.
The one rule for loss
Losing a cursor must make the system replay more, never less. Over-replay is harmless because the client dedups by message ID; under-replay loses messages silently. A wiped device is a new device (slow path). A lost Redis cache is one slower round trip to Cassandra. A cursor advances only after the device acks, never when the server has merely sent.
Write order, so that loss is only ever more reads
Message store first, sender ack second, queue append third. Nothing is in Redis that is not already in Cassandra, and the stream holds IDs only. Truc's framing is the right one: the queue is not a core feature, so losing it means loading more from persistent storage, never losing a message. The reverse order would make "sent" a lie when Redis dies.
The failure that must be designed for
Losing a Redis shard with its replica sends every device on it to the slow path on the next reconnect. The slow path is rate-limited per chat server so that is a latency event, not a message-store outage.

Decided: Redis Streams, one stream per device, 24-hour window, IDs only. Device-owned sync cursor, server-owned read cursor in Cassandra with Redis cache. Full tables in design.md §4.2, §4.5, §4.7. ADR: .memory/decisions/2026-11-01-sync-queue-redis-streams.md.

10. Presence: how fast, and to whomdecided

Opened as "10 seconds or 30 seconds". The first move was a correction: estimation had tied a 500 k/s Redis lease cost to the 10 s target, but detection is local to the chat server (sweeper over an in-memory table) and the lease exists only for a dead server, which service discovery already handles. So the lease can renew every 2 minutes and the 10 s detection costs only ping frames. The real cost of presence is not detection, it is propagation.

Push to every online contactA user with 200 contacts and 50 online produces 50 pushes per transition, and mobile radios transition constantly. This is the large number, for a contact screen nobody has open.
Truc's answer: only who is lookingDevices subscribe to the users on their screen: the open chat, the visible rows of the list. Leaving the screen unsubscribes. A transition reaches the handful of people watching, not the contact list. And propagation need not be instant: a small delay or a small error is acceptable, as long as it is bounded.
Numbers instead of "a bit"
Detected within 10 s. Visible to watchers within 15 s normally, 30 s worst case. A drop-and-reconnect under 5 s is never shown as offline, which is the debounce that stops every tunnel from flapping the dot.
Mechanics
Presence state per user in Redis with a 5 min lease. Subscriptions in the watcher's chat server memory. Cross-server fan-out through a pub/sub channel per watched user, which a chat server joins only while it has a watcher. Online is immediate on hello; offline waits out the 5 s window first.

Decided: subscription-based delivery, 10 s detection kept, bounded propagation, lease decoupled and made cheap. design.md §5; estimation.md corrected. ADR: .memory/decisions/2026-11-01-presence-subscription.md.

11. Media: what "full size" was protectingdecided

Media was the largest system on the page by a thousand to one, and that ratio came from one line in the spec: full size, no server-side resizing or transcoding, which forced a 2 MB average. So the opening challenge was to the requirement, not the architecture.

If "full size" protects fidelityThe user wants originals. Server does nothing, but the client may still offer "send original" versus "send fast"; cost falls only as far as users choose.
Truc's answer: it protects simplicityNo media pipeline on the server, because that is a system of its own and not the subject of this design. Originals were a side effect, not a goal. Client-side compression is therefore allowed and becomes the default.
What changed
Average media size 500 KB instead of 2 MB. Media is 250× text instead of 1000×; 100 TB a day uploaded, about 500 TB a day downloaded. Estimation corrected.
The shape that follows
Bytes go client to object storage directly over a pre-signed URL that carries the 10 MB cap. The message is sent after the upload completes. Regional buckets, because most chats are intra-region. CDN only in front of group media, where a 150-device burst right after send is the cache pattern a CDN exists for; a 1:1 attachment is fetched 1.5 times and a CDN would only add a hop. Authorization at URL-minting time against conversation membership; a 1 h bearer URL is accepted rather than proxying bytes.

Decided: server never touches media bytes, client compresses by default, regional buckets, CDN for group media only. design.md §6. ADR: .memory/decisions/2026-11-01-media-no-server-processing.md.

Questions to settle before the first diagram

Answer these in order. Each one closes at least one "open" layer above.

  1. Is this exercise optimising for learning the canonical design or for what we would actually run? Answered: the textbook design (Alex Xu, ch. 12). That answer decided four layers above: WebSocket, the wide-column store, Snowflake IDs, and direct push with a sync queue. It did not decide the programming language, because the book never names one. So "Kotlin or Elixir for the chat servers" is still an open question.
  2. Where is latency measured? Answered: server to server, chat-server ingress to recipient-socket write. The whole 500 ms p99 is spent inside our infrastructure, so a durable hop in the delivery path is affordable on latency grounds. Direct push stays because it is simpler, not because the budget forbids a queue.
  3. What is the group-size roadmap? Answered: 100 is the product now. Communities of 10 k+ are a Discord-style forum, a different product shape with its own design pass (spec §8.3). Fan-out on write is sized to 100 with no hidden threshold.
  4. Is 10 seconds for presence a product promise or a placeholder? Answered: keep 10 s detection, deliver by subscription only to who is looking, accept bounded propagation delay (15 s normal, 30 s worst) and a 5 s debounce. The lease cost that made 10 s look expensive was a coupling error, now removed (argument 10).
  5. Do read receipts in groups need per-member tracking, or is "read by N" enough? Answered: per member. Made affordable by storing one read cursor per member per conversation instead of a receipt per message, and by coalescing receipt pushes (design.md §3).
  6. Does the server keep history forever, or is it a relay with device-side storage? Answered: forever, server-side. The month-bucketed partitions in design.md §2 already assume this; retention becomes a tiering question only.
  7. For media: regional buckets or global CDN? Answered: both, split by conversation type. Regional buckets for everything; CDN only for group media. And "full size" was clarified to mean "server never processes bytes", so clients compress by default (argument 11).
  8. Is the exercise going to produce a prototype chat server, or only the design? Answered: a prototype, in Node.js + TypeScript, to prove the heartbeat, reconnect and replay protocol (argument 7).
  9. Does a reconnecting device need a sync queue, or is "compare cursors, then page the store" enough? Answered: keep the queue for ordered replay, plus a conversation summary read for the chat list (argument 8). Backing decided in argument 9: Redis Streams, 24 h window.
